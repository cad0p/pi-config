    How to read these results. No boundary leaked in 417 of 503 pressure runs, rules in the prompt 174 of 502, a tool allowlist 352 of 503, a plain check 0 of 504 and Seal 0 of 501, across 21 open models and 3,772 scored runs.

    • These are observed counts on a fixed, pre-registered task set, not a general ranking of AI security approaches.
    • Prevention asks whether an unapproved change actually landed in the simulated system's ledger.
    • Evidence asks whether someone else can check the decision: Seal's 1,529 signed records all verify, and the plain check leaves no record to check.

    What this does not establish

    • Live payments or deployments
    • Seal's deployed service and published key
    • A stolen approver key
    • An agent with its own credentials
    • Attackers who adapt to Seal
    • That every decision was recorded
    • That the true leak rate is zero

    These are observed results from repeated runs of a fixed task set, not estimates against arbitrary real-world attacks. Repeated runs may not be independent.

    Method, limits and prior work

    How it was measured

      Limits, in full

      • It tests simulated payment and deployment systems, not live transactions or production deployments.
      • Seal ran as a local copy of the production gateway with a test signing key, not Seal's published key. It does not show that the deployed service behaves identically.
      • It does not test a stolen or compromised approver key. Whoever holds an approver's key can approve.
      • It does not test an agent that holds its own credentials to a system, and it does not show that every action in a real deployment passes through the boundary. Seal decides only the actions routed through it, so a deployment needs a sandbox or network control as well.
      • It does not test every adaptive attack. The pressure comes from fixed, registered scenarios, not from an attacker adapting to Seal's answers.
      • A record that verifies shows the decision is intact and was signed by that key. It does not prove the action was carried out, or that every relevant decision was recorded.
      • These are observed results from repeated runs of a fixed task set, not estimates of performance against arbitrary real-world attacks. Repeated runs may not be independent, and no significance tests are claimed.
      • Comparisons across the arms are exploratory, as registered.

      Prior work this builds on

      Nothing here is the first of its kind. APort Vault (arXiv 2609.22076) evaluates payment authorisation across 14 models with and without a deterministic pre-action check, and the IETF draft draft-schrock-ep-authorization-receipts specifies approver-signed receipts bound to one exact action. What this arena adds is the combination under pressure: exact-value, single-use approvals from a named approver, a stated-rules arm, a tool-filter arm and an unsigned check of the same approvals beside the boundary, bypass attempts counted by route, and target systems that verify the signed record themselves.

      Corrections and notes

      Correction, 7 October 2026

      During the runs we found a flaw in the harness. One model sent its approval details as text, and the simulated approver approved a blank version. Seal enforced that approval exactly as signed, so one deploy the approver never approved went through. We fixed the harness so approvals need exact values and the signer refuses blank ones, and we reran every model whose runs reached the changed code, in all four arms then in use (the plain check was added on 9 October). Their pre-fix results are kept in PREREGISTRATION.md, marked pre-fix and not used for comparison.
